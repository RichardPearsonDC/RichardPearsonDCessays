The 1983 memo by Lt. Col. Wayne McDonnell, declassified and released by the CIA in 2003, is real and publicly available. What follows imagines a plausible-sounding institutional path the idea could have taken, while flagging where the trail actually goes cold. What the document actually is The 1983 memo is not a research report but a literature review. McDonnell, a commander in an Army Intelligence and Security Command detachment, was tasked with assessing whether the Monroe Institute's "Gateway" training - a Hemi-Sync audio program marketed to civilians in Faber, Virginia - had any scientific legs. He answers that question almost entirely by paraphrasing Itzhak Bentov's popular-science book Stalking the Wild Pendulum, along with borrowed ideas from Karl Pribram's holographic brain theory and a scattering of magazine articles. The paper is earnest, internally consistent by its own logic, and built almost entirely on secondary sources rather than original experimentation. It ends not with a finding but with a list of experiments McDonnell thinks should be run. That distinction - assessment versus experiment - matters for anything that follows. The institutional environment that produced it The memo makes sense only in context. By 1983, US Army INSCOM and the CIA had been funding parapsychology research for roughly a decade under various names - most famously the program that later became public as "Stargate," which used remote viewing for intelligence tasking into the early 1990s. Cold War anxiety that the Soviets had a head start in "psychotronics" kept modest funding alive well past the point most participants expected results. Gateway fits this pattern: a small unit evaluates a civilian relaxation-and-meditation product because someone worried an adversary might get there first, and because the low cost of investigating made ridicule a bigger risk than wasted money. That program was formally shut down in 1995, after a CIA-commissioned review (the Hyman/Utts assessment for the American Institutes for Research) concluded there was no scientific case for continuing it operationally, whatever anecdotal successes individual sessions had produced. That closure is the last well-documented data point in this specific lineage. A speculative path from 1983 to 2026 If one wanted to write the sequel nobody has evidence for, the plausible route would not run through a single continuous "Gateway program" surviving in secret for four decades. Programs like this don't survive stigma; they get renamed, absorbed, or quietly defunded, and the people involved move to adjacent work. A more realistic speculative arc looks like this: 1990s: dissolution, not disappearance. After Stargate's 1995 shutdown, the individuals and contractor relationships didn't vanish - some researchers went into private consulting, some into DARPA-adjacent human performance work, some into commercial biofeedback and neurofeedback companies. The Monroe Institute itself kept operating as a civilian nonprofit, continuing Hemi-Sync research and training therapists, veterans' groups, and hospice programs. If a government interest persisted, it would most plausibly have migrated into unclassified "human performance optimization" research rather than remaining an intelligence-collection tool, since the actual intelligence yield (per the 1995 review) had been the weak link, not the institutional appetite for the topic. 2000s–2010s: rebranding as neuroscience. The core Gateway premise - that binaural entrainment can shift EEG coherence and subjective states - is empirically testable in ways the 1983 memo's cosmology is not. A speculative history would have the biofeedback half of the original memo survive and modernize, while the out-of-body/remote-viewing half quietly dies of evidentiary starvation. 2020s: commercial and wellness capture. If any 2026 institution can trace lineage to Gateway, it is more likely a VA wellness contractor or a startup selling a $300 headband than an intelligence directorate. Where the trail actually goes cold. For a classified 2026 program to exist, it would need sustained funding through fifty years of null results on the operationally important claims (accurate remote viewing under lab conditions, verified extra-sensory information collection) - precisely the claims that got the last iteration cancelled in 1995. Nothing in the public record (congressional oversight, FOIA releases, whistleblower accounts) points to that funding having reconstituted. The more mundane and better-supported story is that this document is a curiosity from a specific Cold War moment when institutional risk tolerance for fringe ideas was unusually high, not a blueprint still in active use. Getting more granular: what "consciousness hacking" would actually require If we take the phrase seriously as an engineering problem rather than a mood, it splits into distinct technical lineages - some with real, documented government funding, and some that stay firmly in Bentov-and-holograms territory. Conflating them is exactly how a real research thread (below) gets laundered into an unfalsifiable one (the 1983 memo). 1. Signal reading - getting information out of a brain. The genuinely funded, documented lineage here is EEG-based and, more recently, DARPA's Next-Generation Nonsurgical Neurotechnology (N3) program, which ran roughly 2019–2022 and funded six teams (Battelle, CMU, Johns Hopkins APL, PARC, Rice, Teledyne) to build noninvasive brain-machine interfaces using combinations of optical, acoustic, electromagnetic, and - in Battelle's case - injectable nanoparticle-mediated approaches, explicitly for military purposes like drone control and cyber-defense teaming. This is public, unclassified, and squarely about reading and writing neural signal, not about remote viewing or holographic universes. It's the real 2020s descendant of "consciousness research" in the sense that matters: DARPA's own language is about resolution (down to roughly 1 cubic millimeter of tissue) and latency (under 10 milliseconds), not about accessing the Absolute. 2. Signal writing - pushing a state into a brain from outside. This is where actual entrainment research (the honest core of Hemi-Sync) sits alongside tDCS/tACS (transcranial direct/alternating current stimulation) and transcranial magnetic stimulation, all of which have real peer-reviewed literature, mixed effect sizes, and FDA-cleared clinical uses for depression and a few other conditions. None of the credible literature supports anything like "download information from the universal hologram." What it does support, modestly, is: audio-driven binaural beats can nudge EEG frequency composition in measurable but small ways; direct current stimulation can shift cortical excitability; and none of this has been shown to reliably alter what a person believes, remembers, or decides in a targeted way. The gap between "measurably shifts EEG power in a frequency band" and "manipulates consciousness" is the whole ballgame, and it has not closed. 3. Why the fantasy is nonetheless durable The appeal of imagining a hidden 2026 Gateway program says more about the document's rhetorical structure than about intelligence history. McDonnell wrote with genuine sincerity and real technical vocabulary - quantum mechanics, Planck length, holography - borrowed from real physicists (Bentov, Pribram, Bohm) but assembled into inferences those physicists never endorsed.
