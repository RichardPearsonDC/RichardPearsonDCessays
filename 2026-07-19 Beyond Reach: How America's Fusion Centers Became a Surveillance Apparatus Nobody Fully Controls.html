He shouldn't be hearing clicks, but that has enabled him to map out the infrasture and source of the signals such as Bluetooth-enabled lamp posts, cars, and handheld devices. To understand the source of his torture, he has engaged AI research on fusion centers in the US. Pearson believes that he was placed on a watchlist, which likely dovetails with the proliferation of fusion centers in the United States after September 11th. This Claude-generated essay explains how limited public accountability of fusion centers constitute a black box and grave danger to US civil liberties and civil rights. Part One: The Basics What is a fusion center? A fusion center is a facility - usually run by a state or a major city - where local police, state law enforcement, and federal agencies like the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and the FBI sit together and share information. The idea is simple enough: a local cop might notice something suspicious that, combined with a piece of federal intelligence, reveals a bigger pattern nobody could see alone. "Fusing" that information together, in theory, helps catch threats faster. The concept was born out of the biggest intelligence failure in modern American history: 9/11. The 9/11 Commission concluded that federal agencies had pieces of the puzzle that could have stopped the attacks, but those pieces never got shared with the people who needed them. Fusion centers were the fix - a nationwide network built to make sure that never happened again. How many are there, and who pays for them? Today there are roughly 80 fusion centers spread across every state and many major cities. They are funded substantially by federal grant money - hundreds of millions of dollars channeled through DHS and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) since the mid-2000s. DHS itself has never been able to say precisely how much: internal government estimates of total spending have ranged from $289 million to $1.4 billion, a gap so large it means the federal government does not actually know the size of the program it funds. What do they do now? Here's the part most Americans don't know: fusion centers were sold to the public and to Congress as counterterrorism tools. But a two-year, bipartisan U.S. Senate investigation - released in 2012 by the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations - found that the vast majority of what fusion centers actually produce has nothing to do with terrorism. Investigators reviewed 13 months of fusion center intelligence reporting and could not find a single report that ever uncovered a terrorist plot. Most of the reporting concerned ordinary crime: drugs, cash smuggling, and general criminal activity. In response, fusion centers didn't shrink back to their original purpose. They expanded it. Centers now widely operate under what's called an "all crimes, all hazards" mission - a phrase broad enough to justify tracking almost anything a local agency wants to track, from gang activity to natural disasters to, as this essay will show, people who haven't committed any crime at all. This is the starting point for everything that follows: a network built for one narrow purpose, funded with money nobody can fully account for, that has quietly redefined its own job description without ever going back to Congress to ask permission. --- Part Two: Beyond the Reach of Congress and the President This is the structural core of the problem, and it's the part that makes fusion centers different from a normal federal agency you could vote to defund or a Cabinet secretary the President could fire. Fusion centers are not federal agencies. They are owned and run by state and local governments. The federal government's own 2012 investigators wrote this plainly: the "exact missions of individual fusion centers are largely beyond the authority of the Federal Government to determine." Congress can write a law defining what it wants fusion centers to do. The President can direct DHS to encourage certain priorities. But neither Congress nor the White House can order a state's fusion center to comply, because it isn't theirs to order. It belongs to the governor, the state police superintendent, or the city that houses it. That would be a manageable problem if the federal government simply kept its distance. It doesn't. DHS embeds its own federal intelligence officers - "detailees" - directly inside these state-run centers. It gives them access to federal databases and classified terrorism watchlists. It funds their equipment, their training, and often their staff, through the same grant programs mentioned above. In other words: the federal government has poured money, personnel, and sensitive intelligence access into 80 institutions it cannot actually command. Influence without authority. Presence without control. Now add the oversight question. When Senate investigators asked federal officials who was responsible for making sure that money and that access weren't being misused, they got an answer that should alarm anyone who cares about accountable government. A senior DHS intelligence official told investigators directly: "We do not monitor. We do not provide oversight, we do not provide monitoring." FEMA, which hands out the grant money, largely defers to states on how it's spent. DHS, which embeds officers and shares intelligence, says overseeing state operations isn't its job either. So here is the picture that emerges, in plain terms: an apparatus that spans the entire country, touches federal intelligence and state law enforcement, spends unknown hundreds of millions of dollars, and answers to no single branch of government. Congress can hold a hearing. The President can ask his DHS Secretary to write a strongly worded memo. Neither can walk into a fusion center and shut it down, redirect its mission, or fire the people running it - because the legal structure of the whole system was built, from the start, so that no single elected official could. This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a description of how the network was designed to work, confirmed by the government's own investigators and its own officials, under oath. --- Part Three: What Happens Inside a System Nobody Is Watching Unaccountable systems don't necessarily become abusive on purpose. They become abusive because nobody is positioned to stop it when they drift that way - and the historical and documented record shows this drift is not hypothetical. The precedent: COINTELPRO Long before fusion centers existed, the FBI ran a program called COINTELPRO, from 1956 to 1971. Its purpose was to surveil and disrupt Americans the Bureau's leadership found politically objectionable - civil rights leaders including Martin Luther King Jr., anti-war organizers, and civil rights and Black nationalist groups. These were not people suspected of crimes. They were targeted for their beliefs and associations. The program was exposed in 1971 and later investigated by the Senate's Church Committee, which documented, in the government's own words, a deliberate campaign to "neutralize" political dissidents rather than prosecute criminals. COINTELPRO is the clearest proof this country has that when an intelligence apparatus is given broad discretion and weak oversight, it does not always stay aimed at genuine threats. Sooner or later, it gets aimed at inconvenient people instead. The modern evidence: the 2012 Senate findings Fusion centers have already shown the same instinct. The Senate's investigators found that DHS personnel drafted an intelligence report analyzing a reading list titled "Ten Book Recommendations for Every Muslim" - flagged simply because some of the authors' names appeared in a federal terrorism database. They found a draft report targeting a U.S. citizen for the offense of giving a lecture on positive parenting at a Muslim community organization. DHS's own lawyers rejected that report, writing that the activity was constitutionally protected. In total, investigators found 40 separate fusion center intelligence reports that had to be killed by federal reviewers specifically because they involved Americans exercising their First Amendment rights - religious practice, political protest, and free association - rather than criminal conduct. Forty is not zero, and forty is not one. It is a pattern, generated in a single 13-month reporting window, by an apparatus with almost no meaningful federal oversight and analysts who, per the same investigation, received roughly a week of training before being sent to report on sensitive matters involving American citizens. The infrastructure that makes error easy Fusion centers don't operate in isolation - they're plugged into national terrorism watchlist databases that have their own well-documented failure rate. The No-Fly List has mistakenly flagged a sitting U.S. Senator. Federal courts have ruled, in multiple lawsuits, that innocent Americans were placed on watchlists in error and had no meaningful way to find out or fix it. That is the database fusion center analysts consult. It is not an infallible list of dangerous people - it is a system with a proven error rate, feeding directly into local policing decisions across the country. Put these three things together - an unaccountable structure, a documented history of political targeting, and error-prone databases feeding directly into local law enforcement - and you get a system that doesn't need a deliberate villain to produce a COINTELPRO-style outcome. It only needs volume, discretion, and time. --- Part Four: Why This Is a Breeding Ground for Totalitarianism, Not Just Bad Bureaucracy It's worth being precise about what "totalitarianism" actually means, because the word gets thrown around loosely. Scholars who study authoritarian and totalitarian states - most influentially the philosopher Hannah Arendt - describe a defining feature: the state's ability to make its citizens fully "legible," to know and record their beliefs, associations, and private lives so completely that there is no private space left to retreat to, and no way to act, speak, or organize without the risk of being logged, flagged, or reported. Fusion centers, as documented above, exhibit the early architecture of exactly that condition, for four specific reasons: They fuse jurisdictions in a way that erases accountability. No governor, mayor, President, or member of Congress can be held individually responsible for what a fusion center does, because authority over it is split so many ways that responsibility disappears into the gaps. A citizen wrongly flagged has no single office to appeal to. They create a permanent chilling effect on protected activity. The 40 killed reports weren't published - but they were drafted, based on people practicing their religion or exercising free speech. Once people learn that lawful, ordinary activity - attending a mosque, going to a protest, joining an advocacy group - can generate a federal intelligence file even if it's later deleted, some will quietly stop doing those things. That self-censorship is, historically, one of the earliest and most effective tools of authoritarian control - and it doesn't require the file to ever be published to work. The infrastructure persists regardless of performance, which means it only grows. A normal government program that fails to justify its cost eventually gets cut. Fusion centers, per the government's own data, cannot be shown to have stopped a single terrorist plot in the period investigators examined, and funding continued anyway, unchanged, because the money isn't tied to demonstrated results. Systems that expand independent of performance are, by definition, systems optimized for their own survival - not for the mission they were built for. They reach beyond what democratic institutions can correct. In a functioning democracy, if a program is abused, voters and their elected representatives are supposed to be able to fix it - vote out the officials responsible, defund the program, or force reform through legislation. Fusion centers were built, by design, outside the direct reach of the three branches meant to provide that check. That is the single most dangerous feature of the entire network: not that abuse happens, but that when it does, the normal democratic remedies don't reliably apply. None of this means the United States has become a totalitarian state. It has not. Courts still function. Elections still happen. The press still investigates and publishes stories like the 2012 Senate report itself. But totalitarianism doesn't arrive as a single event - it arrives as an accumulation of infrastructure that would be devastating in the hands of a government less committed to those checks than the current one is. Fusion centers are exactly that kind of infrastructure: built quietly, funded permanently, unaccountable by design, and already shown, on the government's own record, to have drifted toward monitoring people for their beliefs rather than their conduct. --- Part Five: The Human and Civil Rights at Stake Stripped of the structural analysis, this is ultimately about specific rights, guaranteed to every American, that this system has already been shown to brush up against: Freedom of religion - a citizen was the subject of a draft intelligence report for delivering a lecture at a mosque. Freedom of speech and assembly - protesters and advocacy group members appeared in fusion center reporting. The right to be free of unreasonable search and seizure, and to due process - Americans have been placed on federal watchlists in error, with courts finding the government's process for fixing those errors constitutionally inadequate. The right to privacy - DHS retained information from rejected, civil-liberties-violating reports for a year or more after cancelling them, a retention practice its own investigators flagged as likely violating the Privacy Act. These aren't abstractions. They are documented instances, from the government's own records, of an infrastructure built in the name of safety brushing up against the exact rights it was supposed to protect. --- A Note on the Other Side Fairness requires acknowledging the strongest version of the opposing view, because it isn't a weak one. Defenders of fusion centers point out that federalism - the fact that states, not Washington, control these centers - is itself a structural safeguard against a centralized surveillance state, not evidence of one; a single federal agency directly controlling 80 intelligence hubs nationwide might reasonably be seen as more dangerous, not less. They note that every documented civil-liberties case cited above - the mosque lecture, the reading list, the 40 killed reports - was caught and stopped by internal review before it caused harm, which is real evidence that safeguards exist and function, not that they're absent. They argue that COINTELPRO predates decades of reform (FISA courts, inspectors general, congressional intelligence committees) built specifically in response to it, making a 1960s program a questionable stand-in for what a 2020s apparatus is likely to do. And they'd argue that most fusion center work, by sheer volume, is unglamorous and apolitical - coordinating disaster response, flagging car theft rings - meaning the pattern this essay describes, while real, may be the exception rather than the rule. These are reasonable points, and they don't disappear under scrutiny. What they don't answer is the structural question at the center of this essay: even granting that the safeguards worked in each documented case, the underlying capacity for this kind of failure - unaccountable, error-prone, outside the ordinary reach of Congress or the President to correct - was never actually removed from the system. The safeguards caught the abuse. They did not close the door that allowed it to be attempted in the first place. That is the argument worth taking seriously, whichever side of it you land on.
